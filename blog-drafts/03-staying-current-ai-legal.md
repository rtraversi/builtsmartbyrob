# Three Lessons the Legal World Already Learned About AI

The fastest way to use AI well in a law practice is to learn from the mistakes that have already been made publicly. The legal field has been an early — and very visible — testing ground, and a few lessons have emerged that apply to any firm getting started.

Here are the three I keep coming back to.

## 1. AI will confidently make things up

The most famous early lesson came from attorneys who submitted a court filing with case citations that a chatbot had simply invented — complete with realistic-looking names and quotes. The cases didn't exist. The sanctions that followed made headlines, and the lesson stuck.

AI tools generate text that *sounds* authoritative whether or not it's true. They don't "know" the law; they predict plausible language. That makes them excellent for drafting and terrible as an unchecked source of fact.

**What I'd do:** Treat AI output as a first draft from an eager intern who never admits uncertainty. Useful, fast, and *always* verified before it leaves your office — especially anything with a citation, a number, or a name.

## 2. Courts and bar associations are setting rules — and they vary

Ethics bodies have begun issuing formal guidance on generative AI, addressing competence, confidentiality, client communication, and fees. Some courts now have standing orders requiring lawyers to disclose whether AI was used in a filing, and to certify that the output was checked.

The key complication: this guidance is **not uniform**. What's expected can differ by jurisdiction and even by individual judge. The firms that stay out of trouble are the ones that actually check the rules where *they* practice, rather than assuming a national standard exists.

**What I'd do:** Make "what does my jurisdiction currently say about AI?" a recurring check, not a one-time one. It changes.

## 3. The tool matters less than the workflow

It's tempting to obsess over which AI product is "best." In practice, the firms that get real value aren't the ones with the fanciest tool — they're the ones with a clear, repeatable process: which tool is approved, what it's used for, where client data is and isn't allowed, and who's responsible for checking the output.

A mediocre tool inside a disciplined workflow beats a brilliant tool used carelessly every time.

**What I'd do:** Decide on *one* approved setup, document the do's and don'ts in a single page everyone can read, and revisit it quarterly as the tools change.

## The thread that ties them together

Every one of these lessons points the same direction: AI in a law practice is less about the technology and more about *control* — controlling accuracy, controlling where data goes, and controlling the process. Get those right and the specific tool becomes a detail you can swap out as the market shifts (which it will, constantly).

That's the whole reason I keep this blog. The tools will keep changing; the discipline that makes them safe to use mostly won't.
